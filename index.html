Where AI could take the ACMC lab next

Five project directions that pair AI methods with work the Advanced Cementitious Materials and Composites lab already does: UHPC, crumbling foundations, and durability. Each one builds hands-on experience with AI-integrated research workflows and ends in something you would put your name on.

Prepared by Jonathan TrippettDraft for discussionIncludes a working proof of concept

The core idea. Most "AI for concrete" papers train a model on a few hundred published UHPC mixes and stop at a prediction. The ACMC lab has things those papers don't: years of labeled pyrrhotite field cases, petrographic and microstructural image data, and the ability to pour and test what a model proposes. The strongest projects lean on those assets.

Details

What each path involves

A. Crumbling foundation risk triage

Flagship

A model that takes basement wall photos plus cheap metadata (build year, town, distance from the implicated quarry) and returns a priority score for which homes most need core testing. Core testing can cost up to $4,000 per home, and roughly 35,000 homes around Stafford Springs are potentially affected. The lab already holds the rare combination that makes this possible: field photos paired with actual core results. The USGS aerial survey mapping pyrrhotite across Connecticut adds a free geologic layer.

Builds on
Kay's risk assessment framework published in Cement and Concrete Research
AI workflow learned
Image model fine-tuning, combining images with tabular data, calibrated risk scores
Deliverable
Journal paper and a triage tool CRCOG or CFSIC could use
Watch for
Homeowner consent and privacy; addresses reduced to town or grid level

B. Digital petrography, borrowed from pathology

Flagship option

Thin sections and SEM images of concrete pose the same problem as whole-slide pathology images: find the problem particles, measure them, and grade damage in the surrounding matrix. Computational pathology has years of mature tooling for segmentation, stain normalization, and tiling very large images. Very little of it has reached cement science. Pointed at pyrrhotite detection and oxidation staging in the lab's existing micrographs, this becomes a cross-disciplinary methods paper. It can also serve as the lab-side half of Path A.

Builds on
The lab's electrochemical accelerated-deterioration work and its microstructural analyses
AI workflow learned
Image segmentation, expert annotation loops, quantitative image metrics
Deliverable
Methods paper plus an open analysis pipeline
Watch for
Image formats and magnifications vary; needs a few hundred annotated regions

C. An LLM-built open UHPC database

Workflow builder

Data scarcity is the field's real bottleneck. A 2026 group used an LLM pipeline to pull nearly 9,000 records from more than 27,000 papers on blended cement concrete in about an hour. Typical UHPC machine learning studies train on 500 to 900 points. Nobody has done this for UHPC with full fiber detail (type, geometry, volume, bundling), which is exactly where the lab's expertise sits. Kay would supervise the extraction agent and do the expert validation, which is the best hands-on lesson in AI workflows of the five.

AI workflow learned
Agentic literature extraction, schema design, human validation of AI output
Deliverable
Dataset paper (Scientific Data or Data in Brief tier); widely citable
Bonus
Feeds the faculty-publications search system Kay has asked about
Watch for
Publisher terms on full text; extract facts, not copied passages

D. Lab-in-the-loop mix design for sloped-deck overlays

Longer horizon

Not a robot lab. An agent proposes the next few trial batches using Bayesian optimization, the lab pours and tests them, and results go back in. The target is a real, underserved spec: thixotropic UHPC for bridge deck overlays. Only 17 of the projects on FHWA's UHPC map (about 4%) are deck overlays, partly because standard UHPC self-levels and runs off sloped decks. Add New England materials and a carbon limit and the research question is clean: how many fewer trial batches does it take to meet a DOT spec?

Builds on
Kay's TIDC work on early-strength UHPC with local materials; the UHPC bridge repair work with Arash
AI workflow learned
Experiment planning agents, uncertainty-aware models, closing the loop with real tests
Deliverable
Paper with experimental verification, which most ML mix papers lack
Watch for
Needs a semester of lab time and materials budget

E. AI-assisted comparison of state UHPC overlay specs

On-ramp

A structured comparison of how state DOTs specify UHPC overlays, built with AI extraction and checked by an expert. Modest in scope, doable in weeks, and a low-risk way to learn the workflow before committing to a larger build. A first pass is below.

On satellite imagery. Satellites can't see cracking or chemistry, and basement walls aren't visible from orbit. Satellite radar (InSAR) does measure millimeter-scale movement on bridges over time, and a recent study used free Sentinel-1 images to flag a deteriorating bridge out of 14, later confirmed by inspection. Tracking Connecticut bridges repaired with UHPC against unrepaired ones could work, but it leans toward structures. Worth raising with Arash as a possible three-way collaboration.

28-day compressive strength against the UHPC definition

ACI 239 defines UHPC at a minimum specified compressive strength of 150 MPa (about 22 ksi). None of the four overlay specs require it.

No overlay spec meets the UHPC strength definition.Requirements range from 12.5 to 18 ksi. That fits the Nebraska report's point that overlays depend on bond, tension, and durability more than compression, but the specs still lead with compressive strength.

Only one spec tests tensile behavior.New Jersey sets first-cracking and strain-hardening limits. For a thin layer meant to resist cracking, that gap in the other three is a real research question.

Fiber content splits by mix philosophy.Proprietary-style specs call for 3 to 3.25% fiber. Iowa's non-proprietary recipe uses 2%, which matters for cost and is exactly where the lab's fiber efficiency work applies.

Connecticut isn't in this pass.We didn't locate a CTDOT UHPC overlay provision. If one doesn't exist yet, a New England non-proprietary overlay mix is an open lane for the lab.

Next steps

A suggested sequence

  1. Pick an on-ramp. Path E (finish this spec review across more states) or Path C (start the UHPC database). Either produces a small result within weeks and gets the workflow in place.
  2. Inventory the lab's data. What photos, core results, micrographs, and mix logs exist, in what formats, and under what consent terms. This decides whether A or B is the flagship.
  3. Choose the flagship. A has the clearest public impact; B is the most novel methodologically. They combine well.
  4. Bring in Arash if structures fit. Path D and the InSAR idea both overlap with his bridge repair work.
